Asana earns its place. It's good at the work that crosses departments: a launch that needs marketing, design, legal and engineering to move together, with goals above and portfolios around it. Its rules, forms and timelines are mature, and non-technical teams pick it up quickly.

The teams that look elsewhere usually say one of three things. They only use a fraction of it and pay for all of it. Their data needs to live somewhere Asana doesn't run. Or the work is mostly software, and a general-purpose work manager keeps getting in the way of a sprint.

This guide sorts the alternatives by what you use Asana for, because "best" depends entirely on that. We build [Margaya](/asana-alternative), which is on the list, and we've tried to describe it as plainly as the others.

## First, what do you actually use Asana for?

| If Asana is mainly your... | Look first at |
| --- | --- |
| Shared task list for a small team | Margaya, Trello, Basecamp |
| Coordinator for projects across departments | monday.com, Wrike, ClickUp |
| Docs and projects in one place | Notion, ClickUp, Margaya |
| Tracker for software sprints | Margaya, Linear |
| Something you need to host yourself | Margaya, OpenProject |

If you rely heavily on goals, portfolios and workload across many teams, be honest about it. That's where Asana is strongest, and the lighter tools here won't replace it like for like.

## The options in detail

### Margaya

We build Margaya, so read this with that in mind.

**Good fit if** your team wants clear tasks and clear ownership without a lot of process. Every project has a board, a list, a backlog, a spreadsheet, a calendar and a Gantt timeline over the same tasks, and saved views keep the filters you check every day. Extra planning tools are switched on per project only when a team needs them: cycles for sprints, modules for larger pieces of work, estimates, time tracking, and intake, a queue where requests from outside the team wait for review before they reach the board.

Pages give each project docs that people edit together live, with version history and private drafts. Your work and the inbox collect what's assigned to you and what changed. It's MIT licensed, you can run it on your own server with every feature, or use Margaya Cloud, which is hosted in the EU.

**Watch out for:** Margaya has no goals, portfolios or cross-team workload planning to match Asana's. It has no forms builder beyond intake requests and public boards, and no native mobile apps. If your Asana use centres on those, test carefully.

### monday.com

**Good fit if** you want boards shaped around your process rather than around tasks. Its column types, dashboards and automations let operations, marketing and sales teams build their own workflows without help. It's approachable and visual.

**Watch out for:** seats are sold in fixed bundles rather than one at a time, and automation and integration runs are counted against plan allowances. Check both against how you'll actually use it.

### ClickUp

**Good fit if** you want to merge several tools into one. ClickUp covers tasks, docs, whiteboards, goals, chat and many views, and nearly everything can be customised.

**Watch out for:** the breadth has a cost. New members can find it busy, and a workspace without someone setting conventions tends to drift. Decide early which parts you'll use.

### Trello

**Good fit if** your team mostly needs a shared board. Lists, cards and drag-and-drop are as simple as project tools get, and Power-Ups and built-in automation add what's missing.

**Watch out for:** once work spans many boards, or you need dependencies and timelines, you'll lean on Power-Ups and higher plans, and the simplicity starts to fade.

### Notion

**Good fit if** your team thinks in documents. Notion's databases can be shaped into a task tracker that sits inside your wiki, with views, relations and templates.

**Watch out for:** you build the tracker yourself, so you also maintain it. Task-specific features like notifications, sprints and dependencies are thinner than in a dedicated tool.

### Basecamp

**Good fit if** you want calm, opinionated project communication: message boards, to-dos, schedules, docs and Hill Charts in one place, with very little to configure. It works especially well for client work.

**Watch out for:** it deliberately avoids heavier planning features such as custom fields, sprints and detailed dependencies. That's the point of it, but check it fits.

### Wrike

**Good fit if** you're a larger organisation that needs structure: Gantt charts, workload and resource planning, request forms, approvals and proofing for creative work.

**Watch out for:** it's a big tool with a real learning curve, and it's more than most small teams need.

### Linear

**Good fit if** you used Asana to track software work and found it awkward. Linear is fast, keyboard-first and built around issues, cycles, triage and projects.

**Watch out for:** it's built for product and engineering. Marketing or operations teams who shared your Asana workspace may find it a poor fit.

### OpenProject

**Good fit if** you need to host it yourself and want structured project management: work package hierarchies, Gantt charts, time and costs, meetings and a wiki.

**Watch out for:** the interface is heavier than Asana's, and some capabilities are kept for the paid Enterprise edition.

## Test it on real work

A guided demo always looks good. Before you commit, run a real trial:

1. **Move one active project**, with its open tasks and real owners.
2. **Use it for two weeks**, including at least one planning meeting and one deadline.
3. **Check the boring paths.** Adding a task mid-meeting, finding what's overdue, handing work to a colleague, catching up after a day off.
4. **Ask the people who don't love tools.** If they'd keep using it unprompted, it's working.

## Moving off Asana

Asana can export a project as CSV. That file is the easiest way into most of the tools above.

In Margaya, open **Settings** > **Projects** > your project > **General**, choose **Import**, and pick the CSV file. You map each column to a Margaya field, with **Title** required, and a review step shows what will happen before anything is created: how many tasks, which rows will be skipped, and which values don't match, such as a person who isn't in the workspace yet. A status that isn't a column lands in the backlog rather than failing.

Three practical tips:

- **Invite people first.** Assignees are matched to workspace members, so invite the team before you import.
- **Create your columns first.** Statuses are matched to columns by name, so set up the workflow you want before the first import.
- **Import project by project.** One file holds up to 5 MB and 5,000 rows. Smaller imports are also easier to check.

The [CSV import documentation](/docs/core/functional/import-tasks-from-csv) lists every field you can map.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a free alternative to Asana?

Open-source tools such as Margaya and OpenProject's community edition are free to run on your own server. You pay for hosting and the time to maintain it. Most hosted tools here also offer free plans with limits on seats or features.

### Can Asana be self-hosted?

No. Asana is a hosted service. If your data must stay on infrastructure you control, look at Margaya or OpenProject, which both run on your own servers.

### What does Asana do that most alternatives don't?

Goals, portfolios and workload management across many teams are where Asana is strongest. Wrike and monday.com come closest. The lighter tools on this list don't try to match them.

### How long does a move take?

For a small team, a day to import and a couple of weeks to settle in. The import is quick. Agreeing on columns, labels and who owns what takes longer, so do that first.

## The short version

If Asana is coordinating work across departments for you, and goals and portfolios are part of it, it's hard to beat. Wrike and monday.com are the closest hosted alternatives. If you mostly need a clear task list, Trello or Basecamp will feel lighter. For docs and projects in one place, try Notion or ClickUp. For software sprints, try Linear. And if you want an open-source tool you can host yourself, with optional sprints, docs and intake, try Margaya. Whatever you choose, test it on one real project before you move everyone.
